Why has nobody tried this?

How communities become builders.

A club. A mailing list. A study circle. A few people with a shared problem. People have tried this before. Some built useful things, some fell apart.. and there is quite a bit we can learn from both.

The short version: Some people began with a club, others with a shared problem or an actual business. What they had in common was something people could do together. Each example below tells the story, then compares it with the MapleForge ideas discussed so far. The comparisons are our interpretation, not a verdict from the historians or a decision for the community.
The question

Can a community come first?

Can people gather voluntarily, find collaborators and build useful projects or businesses, without the whole gathering becoming one company?

Yes, people have done this. Quite a few different ways, actually. Some became commercial ventures, some built shared public resources, and others created cooperatives. That doesn’t give us a recipe. But it gives us something better to work with than guessing.

Some relevant history, for discussion. This is not meant as a set of rules or a model everyone should follow. These examples and interpretations are here to give us context, ideas and questions to explore together. No community decisions are being made here.

01 / The closest comparison

A place to share.
A chance to build.

01

Homebrew Computer Club

California, United States · 1975 onward

A meeting place that helped people find their next project

People were excited about computers, but most could not just buy a finished home computer. They met, brought what they were making, asked questions and swapped knowledge. In 1976, Steve Wozniak showed the Apple-1 at the club. Some members went on to build businesses. The club did not have to become one company for those businesses to emerge.

What was behind it?

A regular place to meet, working prototypes to look at, and a newsletter that kept people connected between meetings.

Compared with MapleForge

Where it connects
Our idea board and proposed pods could serve a similar purpose: help people discover who wants to make something with them.
What they had
They already shared a fairly specific interest, personal computers. MapleForge members are still finding the interests and projects they share.
Our different starting point
We can connect online and keep proposals visible between conversations. That makes finding people easier, but a post still needs someone willing to act on it.

Something we could try: Hold a small show-and-tell. Each person brings one thing they made or one problem they need help with.

02 to 05 / Different places, different arrangements

There was never just one model.

02

Apache

International · 1995 onward

People had fixes for the same problem, so they put them together

In 1995, people running websites were fixing the same web-server software separately. Eight contributors formed a group, set up a mailing list and combined their fixes. They tested the software on their own servers and released it for others to use. Four years later, they formed a foundation to support the work. A useful project came first; the larger organization followed.

What was behind it?

They had existing code, people who needed it, and a clear way to contribute a fix. Later, individual project committees handled their own technical decisions while the foundation supplied shared support.

Compared with MapleForge

Where it connects
The portal already gives a small team something concrete to build and improve. Other pods could form around similarly specific work.
What they had
Apache’s first contributors knew which software they were improving. MapleForge is a wider gathering with several possible goals, so it cannot assume everybody is on one product team.
Our different starting point
Our board is also trying to help people choose a direction. Apache’s project processes are more useful after a team has chosen what it is building.

Something we could try: Choose one small portal improvement, name its contributors, test it and show the result. Keep the wider group informed without asking everyone to do the work.

03

The Antigonish movement

Atlantic Canada · 1920s and 1930s onward

Neighbours studied the problem, then built something to help

Fishing and farming families were struggling to earn a decent living. Organizers helped neighbours meet in small study groups, often in homes and workplaces. People learned how their local economy worked and what they might do together. Some groups went on to form cooperatives and credit unions. The discussions mattered because they led towards a practical response to a problem people actually shared.

What was behind it?

The groups had help from educators, field organizers and St. Francis Xavier University. Learning and organizing were real work, not something expected to happen by itself.

Compared with MapleForge

Where it connects
Blackknives’ suggestion to ask what people need, then let smaller groups form, has a useful resemblance to these study groups.
What they had
They shared local economic pressures and had organizers helping them learn. MapleForge has varied interests and cannot assume one problem or one solution fits everyone.
Our different starting point
We can gather people across regions and make prototypes quickly. We still need to find out whether the problem is real for the people a project is supposed to help.

Something we could try: Let a few interested members pick one shared problem, talk to people affected by it, and return with one small action they want to try.

04

Mondragón

Basque Country, Spain · 1956 onward

Training, a small factory, then businesses helping other businesses

A technical school helped people gain useful skills. In 1956, five founders started the industrial business Ulgor. More cooperatives followed. The network developed services such as a cooperative bank and training to help its enterprises operate and grow. These were real businesses with customers, costs and people responsible for running them, alongside ways for members to participate in decisions.

What was behind it?

Technical skills, committed founders, production facilities and shared financial support. The network grew through practical institutions, not just agreement with an idea.

Compared with MapleForge

Where it connects
It shows why “community or business?” can be too narrow a choice. People can help each other and still build businesses.
What they had
They built enterprises with ownership arrangements, managers and financial obligations. A MapleForge account or project sign-up does not yet define those things.
Our different starting point
Our proposed pods leave room for different kinds of projects. A team could choose a cooperative, another kind of company, or a free project without making that choice for everyone.

Something we could try: Ask one interested team what it wants to own together, what each person contributes, and how it would handle money before launching a joint business.

05

Amul

Gujarat, India · 1946 onward

Farmers stayed farmers, but took on a shared bottleneck

Milk farmers had little bargaining power. Milk spoiled quickly, and middlemen could dictate what they paid. Farmers organized village societies and a union to collect and process milk together. The shared enterprise tackled a job that was difficult for each farmer to handle alone. They were not simply a collection of unrelated businesses hoping that bigger orders would save money.

What was behind it?

A common product, a shared market problem, and plans for collection, processing and selling. Organizers helped farmers coordinate the effort.

Compared with MapleForge

Where it connects
A useful MapleForge service could solve the same repeated problem for several independent teams, such as finding collaborators or keeping project information accessible.
What they had
The farmers already produced the same perishable product. Our members do very different things. We have to establish what they genuinely need in common.
Our different starting point
We can test a digital shared service on a small scale before building physical distribution. That does not remove the need to check costs or whether anyone will use it.

Something we could try: Ask three active teams what task each keeps repeating. Only build a shared service if the same need actually appears.

What about kibbutzim?

06

Degania

Ottoman Palestine · Founded 1910

A small group chose to share work and daily life

Degania began in 1910, before the State of Israel existed. Its founders chose communal farming and living. But they were not starting without outside help: the Palestine Office and land purchased through the Jewish National Fund were part of the wider settlement effort. It combined a group’s initiative with institutional resources.

What was behind it?

A group committed to farming and living together, access to land, and support from an organized movement. Its political and settlement history is an essential part of the context.

Compared with MapleForge

Where it connects
The narrow comparison is that a willing small group can choose a much closer form of cooperation than the wider network around it.
What they had
They tied their daily lives and livelihoods to one settlement. Joining an online project is a very different level of commitment.
Our different starting point
Our proposed pods let people take part in selected projects while keeping the rest of their lives separate. This example does not establish what ownership or leadership MapleForge should adopt.

Something we could try: Be specific about what joining a team means, and what it does not mean. Do not confuse interest in a chat with agreeing to share a livelihood.

07 to 10 / A few more worth looking at

Closer to a network.
And one that fell apart.

It’s easy to collect success stories and say “see, it works.” That’s a bit too convenient. These next examples include formal agreements, shared resources, and an experiment that didn’t last.

07

Enspiral

New Zealand and beyond · Around 2010 onward

A network of people, with smaller ventures inside it

People working together developed a network that supports people and ventures doing work they care about. Its handbook makes an important distinction: some documents are agreements people are bound by, while others are practical guides that can be changed more easily. Companies and smaller groups can exist within the network without everyone working on the same venture.

What was behind it?

Written responsibilities, ways to change agreements, and people doing the work of maintaining the network. Its handbook makes those arrangements visible.

Compared with MapleForge

Where it connects
This is close to the proposed combination of a shared space, independent pods and optional ways of working together.
What they had
Enspiral has formal roles and relationships. MapleForge’s board is still a place to discuss what people might agree to, not evidence that they have already agreed.
Our different starting point
Our rules, structure, agreements and feature categories could make the distinction visible. A suggestion needs a clear status before anyone treats it as an adopted rule.

Something we could try: Label each item as a suggestion, a team agreement or an adopted shared agreement, and record who actually agreed to it.

Enspiral: the public handbook

08

Fab Labs

An international network of local workshops

Share the workshop, let people build different things

A fabrication lab gives people access to tools for making things. One person might learn a skill while another develops a product. The network’s charter allows commercial projects, while asking users to look after equipment, work safely and share knowledge. A successful business is expected to grow beyond the lab instead of taking over the shared space.

What was behind it?

Useful equipment and practical responsibilities for the people using it. The wider network offers support that one workshop might not have on its own.

Compared with MapleForge

Where it connects
The portal could play part of the role of a shared workshop: a place to find tools, collaborators and help for different projects.
What they had
A Fab Lab provides physical equipment and has a charter governing its use. A forum by itself cannot supply either the equipment or the people needed to do a project.
Our different starting point
Our shared resources can begin online, with demos, documentation and introductions. Teams do not all need to meet in the same physical workshop.

Something we could try: List what the community can actually offer now, who looks after it, and how a team asks to use it.

09

The Rochdale Pioneers

Rochdale, England · 1844

They started with a shop people needed

Twenty-eight working-class people wanted affordable, decent food. They pooled resources and opened a shop owned and run by its members. It started small, with a concrete service people could use. Its values and practices influenced other cooperatives, which copied and developed the approach.

What was behind it?

A specific need, people willing to contribute resources, and agreed ways of running the enterprise. Customers could tell whether the shop was useful.

Compared with MapleForge

Where it connects
A small, ordinary project can give MapleForge something real to learn from. The first success does not have to solve the whole community’s future.
What they had
The shop had a defined purpose and member responsibilities. We are still matching people and ideas, which is an earlier and less committed stage.
Our different starting point
We can try small digital services without opening a shop. But easy setup is not the same as having users or people committed to keeping it going.

Something we could try: Choose one small service with willing participants and define what would make it useful enough to continue.

Co-operative Heritage Trust: the Pioneers

10

New Harmony

Indiana, United States · Owen’s experiment, 1825 to 1827

Money and talented people were not enough to hold it together

Robert Owen bought an existing settlement and tried to create a model community. William Maclure brought support for science and education, and capable people joined. Within two years, the communal experiment had failed. Some scientific and educational work continued afterwards. The overall plan failed, but not everything people did there disappeared.

What was behind it?

There was property, money, talent and a strong vision. Those advantages did not guarantee a workable community. These short accounts do not establish one simple cause of its failure.

Compared with MapleForge

Where it connects
It is a reminder that attracting people and posting good ideas are only a beginning. Working together requires commitments people can actually carry out.
What they had
This was a settlement built around a broad social experiment. MapleForge does not need to make everyone’s projects depend on one all-encompassing plan.
Our different starting point
Independent pods could limit how much one stalled project affects the rest. That is a possible design choice, not a guarantee against conflict or failure.

Something we could try: Let teams keep their work, document what they tried, and stop or change course without declaring the whole community a failure.

Small correction to the romantic version: “people organized themselves” rarely means nobody organized anything. Somebody arranged meetings, kept records, looked after tools, found funding, or helped resolve a disagreement. That work counts too.

This is our reading across the cases above. How that work is shared, and who can make which decisions, still needs to be made clear.

Making sense of it

So.. what can we take from this?

01

A shared purpose can be smaller than a shared company.

People can agree to exchange knowledge and help projects emerge, while leaving each team to choose its own commercial or noncommercial aims.

02

Concrete work gives collaboration something to organize around.

The examples had activities people could actually do: build computers, improve a server, process milk, manufacture products or establish financial services.

03

Structure can grow at the level where it is needed.

A community needs ways to share space and resolve problems. A project may need additional decisions about roles, ownership, money and delivery. Those are related questions, but they need not all have the same answer.

04

Optional templates can help people start.

A library of possible working agreements could save teams effort. Whether a team adopts one, and what it changes, should be explicit. This is a proposal for discussion, not a conclusion established by these histories.

A possible first experiment / open for discussion

Ok, how do we try it?

Maybe we don’t need to settle the entire community’s future before trying one small thing. We do need enough clarity that people know what they’re joining. Here’s a starting point to change, shorten, or ignore if it doesn’t fit.

First, keep three different questions separate.

The gathering
Where do people meet, find each other and share work? Who looks after the shared space, and what decisions can they make?
The project
Who actually wants to make this thing? What are they taking responsibility for? How will this team settle its own decisions?
The shared support
Would several teams benefit from a library of templates, hosting, introductions or someone helping keep records? Who volunteers to maintain that?

No need to pretend these are three separate organizations. They’re just different jobs. One person may help with several.

Before a team starts, answer six plain questions.

  1. What are we making? Something another person could look at or try. “An interview practice session with five sample questions” is clearer than “transform recruitment.”
  2. Who is actually in? Name the people who have agreed to work on it. A reaction emoji or a server membership is not a work commitment.
  3. What can each person realistically do? An hour, a weekend, one design, one conversation with a possible user. Small, honest commitments are useful.
  4. What are we agreeing to about contributions? Discuss credit, sharing, reuse and commercial intentions before people put substantial work in. Don’t quietly assume everyone has the same expectations.
  5. How will this team decide and disagree? Pick a method the participants understand. Also say what happens if someone wants to stop. This page is not choosing that method for them.
  6. When do we look at the result? Set a short review point. Show what exists, say what didn’t happen, and decide whether to continue, change direction or stop.

About that “10 to 15 people” idea.. There’s no magic team size established by these examples. Start with enough willing people to do the actual task. A bigger group can bring more skills and more coordination work. The fit matters more than hitting a number.

Made-up example, not an active MapleForge project

A two-week interview practice pilot

Three volunteers decide to run two practice sessions. One drafts the questions, one organizes the sessions, one collects feedback. They agree in advance how their materials can be shared and what they want to test.

At the end, they show the question set, explain what participants found useful, and decide what to do next. Maybe that becomes a service. Maybe it stays a free resource. Maybe they stop.

The useful outcome is something tested, plus a clearer idea of whether these people want to keep working together. Nobody else in the community has to join the project for that to count.

And the awkward bit: what happens when it stalls?

Before making another form, ask what’s actually missing. A person with time? A specific skill? A decision? Access to something? Or does nobody really want to do this particular project?

A template helps with some of those problems. It won’t supply an interested teammate. A chat can introduce people, but it won’t make their commitments for them either.

It’s also fine for an experiment to end. Keep a short note of what was tried, what exists and what someone else would need to continue. Saves the next person starting the exact same conversation from zero.

A few questions worth bringing back to the group
  • Which idea has people willing to work on it this week?
  • What would make a second person comfortable joining?
  • Which decisions belong to that team, and which affect everyone sharing the space?
  • Could the parent community offer something useful without taking control of the project?
  • What would we accept as a useful result, even if no business comes out of it?
A question to build around

What is the smallest useful thing
we could do together?

These examples show that collective effort can produce real outcomes. They do not prove that every community will succeed, that leadership is unnecessary, or that one model fits everyone.

A shared space. A few willing collaborators. A concrete first step. What would yours be?
